# Abstract and Introduction

## Abstract: A Decision-Centered Sequence

Build the abstract from moves, not a fixed sentence count:

1. **Context and decision:** identify the transport system and decision that matter.
2. **Gap:** state what current evidence or planning practice cannot yet do.
3. **Purpose/questions:** state what the study estimates, explains, compares, or optimizes.
4. **Evidence and method:** name the study area, data, unit/time frame, and method at the level needed to judge credibility.
5. **Findings:** report the most decision-relevant quantitative results, heterogeneity, spatial pattern, or trade-off.
6. **Contribution/implication:** state what understanding or decision capability changes, with an appropriate boundary.

For prospective low-altitude studies, specify the forecast/scenario year and avoid “there will be” when the number is conditional. Prefer “under the stated 2030 scenario, the model estimates...” or equivalent.

Do not use the abstract to list every procedure. A named model matters only if the reader also learns the problem it solves and the evidence it produces.

## Introduction: Travel Behavior Route

Use this route when the unit of interest is a traveler, household, trip, or choice:

1. Explain the transport service or behavioral phenomenon and its system relevance.
2. Identify the high-level behavior or need that remains unobserved or incompletely measured.
3. Explain consequences of that gap for at least one research or decision stakeholder.
4. Narrow to precise research questions: what, when, where, how much, for whom, under what conditions, or why.
5. Preview how the data and analytical design make those questions answerable.
6. State contributions by outcome, not by procedure alone.

Strong gap forms include:

- effects of a service are well studied, but underlying needs or mechanisms are not;
- average preferences are known, but timing, context, or population heterogeneity is not;
- specific alternatives are studied separately, but their interdependence or overall choice set is not;
- attitudes are observed, but their relationship to actual or stated choices is unclear.

## Introduction: Low-Altitude/Planning Route

Use this route when the unit of interest is a network, infrastructure system, facility set, or emerging mode:

1. State the plausible transport function of the emerging system without opening with hype.
2. Explain the urban-system stakes: accessibility, commuting, land use, network integration, environment, safety, acceptance, or equity.
3. Identify the focal planning decision and the actors responsible for it.
4. Show the first mismatch, such as technology development versus deployment knowledge.
5. Show the second mismatch, such as feasible supply versus spatial demand, or efficiency versus social impacts.
6. Explain why the gaps must be addressed together.
7. State the research objective and contributions.

For vertiport or facility location papers, introduce the facility only after readers understand the service and planning stakes. Explain how location changes access, coverage, cost, transfers, exposure, or acceptance.

## Contributions

Select only contributions the paper actually supports:

- **Conceptual:** reframes the decision or connects previously separated constructs.
- **Methodological:** creates or adapts an analytical framework that changes what can be estimated or decided.
- **Empirical:** provides credible evidence for an underexamined behavior, population, place, or emerging system.
- **Planning/decision-support:** exposes trade-offs, thresholds, priorities, or implementable rules.
- **Policy:** identifies a supported intervention mechanism and affected stakeholders.

Write each contribution as `prior limitation -> what this study adds -> why that addition matters`. “Uses method X in city Y” is not sufficient by itself.

## Introduction Check

- Is the central decision problem visible before technical detail?
- Is the gap supported by the literature rather than asserted?
- Do the research questions map to later results?
- Are demand, accessibility, equity, sustainability, safety, and acceptance defined rather than treated as slogans?
- Are contributions distinct from objectives and findings?
- Does the introduction avoid promising causality, validation, or generalizability that the design cannot deliver?
